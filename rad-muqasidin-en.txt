Introduction
The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) continued to receive revelation from his Lord, Exalted is He, and he would inform his companions (may Allah be pleased with them) about it, and they would receive it from him with listening and obedience. Sometimes they would be astonished, as when the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) spoke to them about a cow that speaks, and they were amazed and said: "A cow that speaks?" He said: "I believe in this, I and Abu Bakr and Umar"(1). At other times, they would become angry, as when the Quraysh were upset about giving the people of Najd and leaving them(2), or they would hesitate at times, such as their hesitation in slaughtering and shaving after the story of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah(3). However, despite all of this, the command of Allah does not change due to their astonishment, nor their anger, nor their hesitation (may Allah be pleased with them), even though such occurrences were rare and not the norm. In the end, everyone submits to the command of Allah, Exalted is He.
There is no report of them saying what those with sickness in their hearts said: "What did Allah intend by this as an example?"(4), nor did they ask Him, Exalted is He, why did You do this? And Allah, Exalted is He, said: "He is not to be questioned about what He does"(5). The matter remained in this state until, after several centuries and the passing of the best early generations, some began to ask this question: What did Allah, Exalted is He, intend by legislating this ruling? And why did Allah, Exalted is He, decree this ruling? Then they looked into the branches of the Sharia. If

First - The concept of objectives (maqasid) according to those who affirm them:
The researcher will limit himself to one comprehensive definition, which is "what the Legislator intended to achieve through the rulings or a group of them"(11).
The one who proposed this definition pointed out its benefit in being both comprehensive and restrictive, as he defined it by "what the Legislator intended to achieve" so that everything intended by the Legislator in legislation is included in the definition, whether it pertains to the interests of the obligated individuals or not, such as trials. He did not say "its achievement for the people," but rather "its achievement" alone, so that the definition encompasses what was intended by the legislation to be achieved for them, such as interests and trials, or what is to be achieved from them, such as compliance and understanding. The term "rulings" refers to the very texts that contain the obligations(12).
It is clear from this definition, which is close to al-Shatibi's definition of the types of objectives and derived from it, that what the Legislator intended to achieve can be of four types: it may either contain a benefit for the servant, and this benefit may be a worldly benefit or a purely otherworldly benefit, as al-Shatibi himself explained when discussing the objectives, or it may be a trial from Allah, Exalted is He, and this trial may be contrary to what the servant perceives as beneficial or

Secondly— why did these people speak of what is called the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia)?
To simplify, why did they say that what Allah, Exalted is He, intended with His legislation of rulings is what His servants see as beneficial for them?
The answer to this question is based on two premises that lead to this conclusion:
First: They said that the concepts of good and evil are only for the intellect. Thus, whatever the intellect perceives as good is good, and whatever it sees as beneficial is the benefit. If the intellect acknowledges that something is a benefit and the textual evidence contradicts that, the intellect takes precedence, and the text is interpreted or deemed weak. This is the doctrine of the Mu'tazila(16).
Second: That Allah, Exalted is He, does not command except what is good in our minds, for the actions of Allah are rationally justified. We examined the Sharia to extract a purpose or intention behind the Sharia and found that this is the benefit of the servant. Therefore, the intention of Allah, Exalted is He, in all His commands is the benefit of the servant, and the intellect must first perceive it as good. The Mu'tazila consistently applied this doctrine in beliefs, acts of worship, and all branches of the Sharia.
Some limited their focus to the realm of actions, excluding beliefs and acts of worship. They examined the texts of the Sharia, whether they were established by conjecture or indication, and whatever contradicted that from their perspective of it being beneficial was rejected.

The text is cold, and among these is al-Tufi the Hanbali(17). The contemporary proponents of the objectives (maqasid) followed him with varying degrees, and they will be the subject of critique, if Allah wills.
Al-Tufi formulated his theory in the early eighth century, and Dr. Muhammad Abu Zahra said about him: "He was a Shiite." The summary of his theory is as follows:
1- The intellect is capable of discerning benefits and harms except in acts of worship, as they are the rights of the legislator.
2- Benefit is a legal proof independent of the text and does not rely on the testimony of the texts for its validity.
3- Abandoning the text is permissible if the intellect perceives a benefit in opposing it, provided that the text is uncertain in its authenticity or meaning(18).
As for the third group - the Malikis and those who agreed with them - they narrowed the scope further. Some of them relied on the notion that the intellect can determine what is good and bad, and that it can perceive benefits in matters of transactions but not in acts of worship. However, the Ash'arites among them held that the determination of good and bad belongs solely to the Sharia, yet they also acknowledged what is known as public interest (maslaha mursala). However, this public interest is not a legal proof in and of itself; rather, the Sharia must testify to the category of this interest, even if it does not testify to its specific instance. If the Sharia does not testify to its category, it is considered an invalid interest and is not permissible.

The Concept of Objectives (Maqasid) According to al-Shatibi
Introduction
It has become common among many contemporary scholars, when using the concept of objectives (maqasid), to assert that the primary objective of the legislator is the welfare of the servants, and that the welfare of the servants is known through the customary practices among people, and what people perceive as welfare. This concept has often been attributed to al-Shatibi. Did al-Shatibi truly intend what they claim and perceive? In this research, I attempt to find an answer to this question by presenting al-Shatibi's views on this topic, and may Allah grant success. However, before beginning this research, the researcher wishes to present his perspective regarding the meaning of the objective, the cause, and other related matters, as clarifying the point of contention will, with Allah's permission, remove the ambiguity.
First: The difference between the cause (illa), the reason (sabab), the purpose (gharaḍ), the sign (ʿalama), the meaning (maʿna), the public interest (maslaha), and the wisdom (hikma)
Ibn Hazm (may Allah have mercy on him) says:
“Abu Muhammad said, if someone were to say, you deny the statement regarding causes (al-‘ilal) and you affirm the existence of reasons (asbab), what is the difference between the two matters?
The answer, and with Allah's help, is that the differences between the cause (illah), the reason (sabab), the sign (alamah), and the objective (gharaḍ) are clear and evident distinctions. All of them are valid in their respective contexts, and none of them necessitate reasoning in the Sharia or a ruling by analogy (qiyas) at all. We say, and with Allah's help,
The cause (illa) is the name for any attribute that necessitates a certain matter with absolute necessity. The cause is never separate from its effect (ma'lul) at all, just as fire is the cause of burning and snow is the cause of cooling, where neither exists without the other at all, and neither precedes the other nor follows it.
the cause is everything that the agent (al-mukhtar) does for which, if he had wished, he could have refrained from doing, such as anger that leads to victory; thus, anger is the cause of victory. The cause does not necessitate the effect (al-masbub) necessarily, as it exists prior to the effect that results from it. As for the objective (al-gharaḍ), it is the matter that the doer aims for and intends to accomplish, and it is necessarily after the action. The objective of victory is to extinguish and remove anger. The removal of something is distinct from its existence; thus, the removal of anger is different from anger itself. Anger is the cause of victory, while the removal of anger is the objective in victory. Therefore, it is correct that each meaning we have mentioned is distinct from the other, and victory lies between anger and...

The statement of Allah, “Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship” { يريد الله بكم اليسر ولا يريد بكم العسر } (263) is undoubtedly true, but what ease are they talking about, and what hardship has Allah removed from us in His legislation? Is it every hardship? Certainly not. For instance, fasting the obligatory fasts in the heat of the day, performing ablution with cold water, fighting, and enduring the preservation of the religion despite trials and hardships, as Allah said { وَإِن كَانَتْ لَكَبِيرَةً إِلَّا عَلَى الْخَاشِعِينَ } (264). Allah did not permit His Messenger to change the law to align with the desires of the polytheists, while He knows the extent of the hardship and difficulty that would befall him and his companions as a result. All of this leads to the conclusion that the hardships of the Hereafter and the hardships after death are the greatest hardships that Allah intended to lift from us, and these major hardships can only be lifted by implementing the commands of Allah, Exalted is He.

From this text, it is clear that the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) according to al-Shatibi are divided into primary objectives, which he means, and Allah knows best, is the objective of creating people for worship or the objective of the servant's compliance with the divine command, as will be discussed, God willing. However, after the legislation from Him, Exalted is He, there are what are called secondary objectives, which are related to the one obligated (mukallaf). These objectives may either align with the intent of the legislator or contradict it. If they align, they are permissible and beneficial; if they contradict, they are prohibited and harmful.
The concept of objectives according to al-Shatibi
He says: "The objectives that are considered are of two types: one pertains to the intent of the legislator, and the other pertains to the intent of the obligated. The first is considered from the perspective of the legislator's intent in establishing the Sharia from the outset, from the perspective of his intent in making it understandable, from the perspective of his intent in establishing obligations based on it, and from the perspective of his intent in bringing the obligated under its ruling; these are four types"(274).
Subhi Taha says: "Al-Shatibi clarified that the objectives of the legislator from the Sharia are four types: his intent in establishing the Sharia from the outset, his intent in making it understandable, his intent in establishing obligations based on it, and his intent in bringing the obligated under its ruling. This is not merely al-Shatibi's opinion, but it is established among all; no one can deny it. For everyone agrees that the Sharia aims to bring benefits to people and also aims to make clear to the addressed what it contains of rulings and to obligate them to those rulings and their compliance with them. These objectives have different realities; some are for bringing benefits to people, some are a test for them to distinguish the pure from the impure, some are for their understanding so that the proof may be established against them, and some are their actions, which are obedience and compliance with what is stated in the Sharia. It is known that not all of this is beneficial for people, as some people do not successfully pass this test, some do not comply, and some go astray in what they understand. Allah, the Exalted, says: {And We did not send any messenger except with the language of his people to make clear to them. Then Allah leads astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills. And He is the Exalted in Might, the Wise} (4) [Ibrahim] (275). Thus, the benefit is not in the intent of obligation, nor in the intent of compliance, nor in the intent of understanding, but rather the benefit is in the very act of compliance, in benefiting from what is understood, and in successfully passing the test. Therefore, it is established that the realities of the Sharia are different, and thus it is difficult to provide a definition that encompasses them. This may be another reason added to what I mentioned earlier for the lack of definitions by the predecessors regarding the objectives of the Sharia, and it may be the reason why contemporary definitions have focused on defining only the first type, which is what the phrase 'objectives of the Sharia' generally refers to among the majority of scholars of Sharia who are not specialized in the science of usul. Thus, it can be said that contemporary definitions are not definitions in

The truth about the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) is that it is merely a definition of one type of them. If we wanted a definition that encompasses everything that falls under the term objectives of the Sharia, we would say that the objectives of the Sharia are: "what the legislator (al-shari) intended to achieve through the rulings or a group of them." I said "what the legislator intended" so that the definition includes everything the legislator intended through legislation, whether it is benefits for the obligated (mukallaf) or not, such as trials (ibtila). I did not say "its attainment for the people," but rather I said "its attainment" alone so that the definition includes what was intended by the legislation to be achieved for them, such as benefits and trials, or what is to be achieved from them, such as compliance and understanding. The term "rulings" refers to the very texts that include obligations, recommendations, prohibitions, dislikes, and permissions, as is the terminology of the jurists, along with what is related to them from causes, conditions, and impediments (276).
This statement is very important and necessary to correct people's understanding of the objectives that al-Shatibi referred to. Unfortunately, most contemporary scholars have reduced the concept of objectives to just one type, leading many people to believe that the concept of objectives according to al-Shatibi is what they refer to. They even state in their writings about the objectives in their books or articles that one of the first to consider the objectives was al-Shatibi, while al-Shatibi is innocent of their claim, as has been clarified. It is unclear whether this oversight is due to ignorance or ill intent. This has resulted in widespread corruption and statements that have no basis in divine authority, which are in clear contradiction to the Sharia. These individuals did not limit themselves to the types of objectives mentioned by previous scholars, but they also spoke of what they called the renewal of objectives, thus they began to derive new objectives for Allah, Exalted is He, as if the best generations were unaware of this goodness that others have reached through their derivation of Allah's intent and purpose. They do not realize that they are attributing negligence to the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him), claiming that he knew those objectives of Allah for which they alter the rulings, yet he did not convey them to us despite their importance until these individuals came and discovered them. They criticize the earlier scholars for their lack of interest in extracting what they termed the general objectives (277). Thus, they invalidated the punishment for apostasy by what they called the objective of freedom of belief, and they permitted women to marry themselves in violation of the hadith: "There is no marriage except with a guardian" (278), claiming the objective of the leniency of the Sharia in changing rulings due to changing customs. They also permitted women to lengthen their hair and to use dental embellishment in violation of the hadith: "Allah has cursed those who lengthen their hair and those who create gaps in their teeth for beauty" (279), for the same previous objective. Moreover, they even permitted explicit usury with the wealthy under the guise of development and investment, claiming that the objective of the Sharia is to guide creation morally and educationally, and that there is no contradiction between this and lending to the wealthy with interest. Thus, they interpret the texts prohibiting usury under this overarching objective, limiting its prohibition to the poor only (280). There is no power and no strength except with Allah.
